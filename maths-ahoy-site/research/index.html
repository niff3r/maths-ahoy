Research

My PhD looked at students peer reviewing maths work using comparative judgement.

Usually, an assessor judges each piece of work on its own, often against a rubric. In comparative judgement, the assessor looks at two pieces of work side by side and simply decides which one is better. Repeat that with lots of different pairs, and all those decisions combine into a ranking of every piece of work, from strongest to weakest.

Which one's better?

Here are two students' solutions to the same inequality problem. Which one do you think is better? Click to choose.

Students who chose A often said it was neater, with clearer handwriting, and that it explained the steps in words.

Students who chose B often said it was easier to follow than A.

That's comparative judgement in action. In my research, I looked at the effect it had on students' learning. When students then solved the exact same problem themselves, did those who had compared worked solutions side by side do better, worse, or the same as students who were shown the same worked solutions one at a time?

What I found

Comparative judgement had worked well in subjects like Design and Technology, but had barely been tried in maths. Across three studies, I looked at what students think about as they compare, and whether comparing helps them learn.

The results depend on the context, but two differences stood out.

Comparing two solutions is easier

When students look at two solutions side by side, they can use each one to judge the other. Is this step clearer than that one? Is this method quicker? Does this explanation make more sense? Every decision is a comparison between two things they can see, so they don't need to already know what a perfect answer looks like.

Judging one solution on its own is harder

With only one solution in front of them, students have to rely on their own sense of what good work looks like. That's harder, because it draws on prior knowledge and experience. If students haven't seen good work of this kind before, they have nothing to judge it against.

However, when students judged one solution at a time, they paid closer attention to accuracy. That may make it a useful approach for building procedural skills.

What's still open

We still know very little about how comparative judgement works with younger students, or with students who are new to it.

It's also unclear how much support to give. Does showing students the correct answer first build their confidence, or get in the way?

And the biggest question: does comparing change what students understand, or mainly how clearly they explain it?
