Stationery maths

Paper clips, rubber bands, and strips of paper. What happens when you pull the strip tight?

What students do

Students clip paper clips onto a folded strip of paper, add a rubber band, pull the strip tight, and watch what happens. Put a paper clip on one side of the loop and it ends up linked to the rubber band. Put it somewhere else and it doesn't.

So students experiment like scientists. They gather data, record what happens, and start making guesses. What if the clip goes here? What if we make a new loop? What does one paper clip plus one paper clip equal? Before long the question becomes: can we predict what will happen without testing it?

The maths behind it

This is topology: the maths of shapes and how they connect. Students start by testing, but the real goal is to predict. Can you look at where the clips and bands sit on the strip and know what will happen before you pull? Finding the rules that let you do that is a kind of computation: working out an answer without having to do the experiment.

The activity is based on the paper Paperclip graphs by Steve Butler, Erik Demaine, Martin Demaine, Ron Graham, Adam Hesterberg, Jason Ku, Jayson Lynch, and Tadashi Tokieda.
