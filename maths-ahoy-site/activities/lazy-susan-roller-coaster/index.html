The lazy Susan roller coaster

Build a roller coaster out of matchsticks. Is it safe? Is it exciting enough to ride?

What students do

Students design a roller coaster on grid paper, using matchsticks as straight sections of track. Then they draw a gradient graph of their ride, showing how steep the track is at every point along it.

Finally, they read values off their gradient graph to give the roller coaster a safety rating. Too steep and it's dangerous. Too flat and nobody wants to ride it. Can they design a ride that meets both the safety and the excitement requirements?

The maths behind it

Gradient measures how steep something is. A gradient graph, also called a derivative graph, shows how that steepness changes all the way along the track. Building the track by hand makes the link between a shape and its gradient something students can see and feel, and it's a first step towards one of the big ideas in calculus.

I wrote about this activity in You don't make friends with salad, Vinculum, 50(4), 4–6 (2013).
