Crazy cubes

Four cubes, four colours. Line them up so every side of the row shows each colour once. How hard can it be?

What students do

Students get four cubes, each with faces in four colours, and try to arrange them in a row so that the top, bottom, front, and back each show one face of every colour. It's a classic puzzle, sold in the 1960s as Instant Insanity, and it's deceptively tricky.

Trial and error gets students started, but not very far. Then they learn to draw each cube as a network graph, combine the four graphs into one, and use it to solve the puzzle.

The maths behind it

Graph theory is the maths of networks: dots joined by lines, called edges. Here, each colour becomes a dot. A cube has three pairs of opposite faces, and each pair becomes an edge joining its two colours. If both faces in a pair are the same colour, the edge loops back to its own dot.

Draw all four cubes this way, label each edge with its cube's number, and put everything on one graph. The puzzle then becomes a search for two smaller graphs inside the big one: one for the front and back of the row, and one for the top and bottom. Each must use exactly one edge from every cube, every colour must appear exactly twice, and the two can't share an edge.

I wrote about this activity in Crazy cubes: Positioning incorrect answers as valuable, Australian Mathematics Education Journal, 4(2), 22–27 (2022).
