Resurrecting the deck

Shuffle a deck of cards, again and again. How many shuffles until it comes back to exactly where it started?

What students do

Shuffling looks like it should scramble everything, but with the right kind of shuffle, do it enough times and the deck comes back to its original order.

Here's one way to shuffle, called an out-shuffle. The deck is split exactly in half, the two halves are interleaved one card at a time, and the top card stays on top.

Starting small

Students first learn to resurrect smaller decks of 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 cards, practising different types of shuffle. They shuffle, record the order after each shuffle, and count how many shuffles it takes to get the deck back.

Permutations

Next, students learn permutation notation to describe where each card goes. In an out-shuffle of eight cards, the cards in positions 1 and 8 stay put, while the others travel in two loops: 2 goes to 3, 3 to 5, and 5 back to 2, and 4 goes to 7, 7 to 6, and 6 back to 4. In cycle notation, that's (1)(2 3 5)(4 7 6)(8).

Both loops have length 3, so after 3 shuffles every card is home. Students use this idea to work out how many shuffles it takes to bring a full deck of 52 cards back to the start. It's fewer than you think!

The maths behind it

Each perfect shuffle moves every card to a new position by the same rule, so the shuffle is a permutation. Breaking it into cycles, and finding when all the cycles line up again, leads to lowest common multiples.
